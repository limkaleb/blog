[East of Eden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_of_Eden_(novel)): What I'm Taking Away From It

I finally get why people call East of Eden [Steinbeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Steinbeck)'s masterpiece. On the surface it's a big family saga set in California. Underneath, it's Cain and Abel played out over two generations. Here's what stuck with me.

1. **You're not doomed.** The whole book hinges on one Hebrew word: timshel, "thou mayest." Not "you must," not "you will," but you may. Whatever darkness you're born with, you still get to choose what you do with it. That's freeing and kind of terrifying at the same time.

2. **Your parents aren't your destiny.** Cal is convinced he inherited his mother's evil. Spoiler: the fact that he worries about it, feels guilty and keeps trying to be better is exactly what makes him different from her.

3. **Rejection hits hard.** Again and again, a son offers his father something and gets turned away while his brother gets the love. Every time, that hurt turns into something ugly. Steinbeck basically says most of the bad in the world starts with someone feeling unloved.

4. **"Perfect" people break easily.** The "good" characters, Adam and Aron, live in idealized bubbles, and when reality hits, they shatter. Cal's messy, hard-earned goodness is the kind that actually lasts.

5. **The wisest people are the overlooked ones.** Samuel, a broke farmer, and Lee, the family's servant, are the real philosophers of the book. Pay attention to them.

Bottom line: you can't pick where you come from or how you were loved, but you can pick who you become. Timshel.

Read it if you haven't. It's long, but it's worth it.
